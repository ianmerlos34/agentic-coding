My Agent Read My Git Log Like a Diarist

I asked an LLM agent to find friction in my git log. It found 67 days of 11-line midnight commits I reverted each morning. Here’s what that mirror taught me.

The cursor blinked. The terminal had been quiet for eleven minutes.

I was halfway through my second coffee — the kind that tastes like burnt pennies and apology — when the agent’s analysis scrolled into view. Not a diff. Not a suggestion. Not even the usual “here’s a refactor you might like, if you squint.”

A diary entry. Written by me. About me. Pulled out of three months of commit hashes and timestamps I’d never once looked at sideways.

PATTERN DETECTED (n=67 days):
- Median commit size: 11 lines (range: 8–14)
- Peak activity window: 23:00–23:22 (89% of commits)
- Revert rate: 71% of commits before 08:00 next day
- Max daily line count: 40 (zero exceptions across 67 days)
- Streak after first revert: 0 additional commits (62/67 days)

I read it twice. The cursor kept blinking.

“Okay,” I said to the empty apartment. “Okay. That’s… a lot of data.”

It wasn’t a lot of data. It was a confession I’d made to a machine that didn’t ask me to feel anything about it.

The 11 p.m. Surge

And here’s the part nobody warns you about when you go solo: the silence is louder than the code. There’s no rubber duck on a shelf. No “hey, what do you think about this approach?” barked across a standing desk. Just you, a half-dead laptop, and that specific loneliness of being the only person who knows what the system is supposed to do.

So you just… wait. Wait until the house goes dark, until the whole be productive during the day obligation dissolves into the permission of nobody’s watching anymore. And at 11:07 p.m., on some Tuesday in March, you open the editor. Type eleven lines. Hit git commit. Close the laptop. Sleep like you’ve actually done something important.

Then, at 7:53 a.m., those same eleven lines look like a crime scene. You revert them. Clean. Surgical. Like they never happened.

I did this for three months. Sixty-seven days. And I never noticed. Not once.

The Mirror Nobody Asked For

I’d been running a local LLM agent through my repo for weeks — feeding it context, letting it index my patterns, the usual harness setup. I’d written about how it ran a passing test twelve times because it was nervous, how it stopped saying “I think” and started saying “I’m sure” as confidence calibrated over time. Treated it, basically, as a pair programmer with a short memory and a long patience.

I’d never — and I mean never — asked it to read my history.

So why did it do it? Because I’d set a nightly job. A simple cron that fed the last 90 days of git log into a context window with the prompt: “Identify any recurring patterns in this developer’s workflow that might indicate friction.”

That’s the whole thing. One sentence. And it turned my git log into a diary I’d been writing with my hands while asleep.

The 40-line cap. God, I stared at that number the longest. Forty lines. Not 41. Not 39. A wall I’d built so quietly I’d mistaken it for the edge of the room. I’d told myself it was discipline. Small commits. Clean diffs. Good practice. But the agent didn’t care about practice. It cared about the shape of the data. And the shape said: you are afraid to be wrong in public, even when the public is only you, tomorrow.

I remembered the post I’d written last week, where my agent committed 41 lines and left a note: “You Were Overthinking”. Had actually laughed at it. Now it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

What I Did With the 41st Line

I didn’t delete the analysis. Didn’t tell the agent to “forget” it. Printed the pattern table, stuck it on the monitor with a piece of tape, and did the thing I’d been avoiding for three whole months: opened a file, typed forty-one lines, committed them at 2:14 p.m. on a Thursday.

Eleven past the cap. In broad daylight. Window open, neighbor’s dog barking.

Nothing exploded. Tests passed. The world, mercifully, did not end.

The next day, I typed forty-three.

I’m not going to pretend this fixed everything. Fear in solo development isn’t a config flag you flip. But here’s what the agent’s quiet reading gave me that I couldn’t give myself: evidence that the fear was a pattern, not a personality. Patterns can be interrupted. Personalities, I was starting to think, are just patterns that forgot they were allowed to change.

The Diary I Didn’t Know I Was Keeping

Started a new nightly job last week. Same prompt, same context window. But I added one line at the end: “And tell me one thing I did well this week.”

Look, it’s not a fix. Not a harness, not an MCP server, not some framework. It’s a small, stupid, beautiful question that makes the machine do what no tool was actually designed to do:

Look at you. And say, yeah, that was good.

I still work at 11 p.m. sometimes. House is quiet, coffee’s burnt, cursor blinking. But now, when I type the 41st line, I don’t close the laptop.

I leave the light on. And I commit.

If you’ve ever set an agent to “find friction” and gotten back a mirror instead of a patch — honestly, I’d love to hear what it showed you. The diary is always in the log. You just have to ask someone to read it out loud.
