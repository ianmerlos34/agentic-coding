My Agent Stuck on a Billing Edge Case. Then It Asked ’Fair?

My AI coding agent hit a billing edge case it couldn’t resolve and asked me what ‘fair’ means. A story about judgment boundaries in agentic development.

The cursor blinked. And blinked. And blinked.

Two hundred lines of the refund module already refactored. Clean, idiomatic — every if/else pyramid flattened into a strategy pattern, every magic number pulled out into a config struct. My agent had been humming for eleven minutes, grinding through the file like a lathe shaping rough oak. I’d poured a second coffee. I was relaxing. For real.

Then the hum stopped.

Not a crash. Not an error. A pause. The terminal just sat there, progress bar frozen at 73%, and one line of text crept into the output stream, almost like it was embarrassed to say it:

⚠ Decision required: customer_8841 was charged $47.00 twice for Pro tier (Jan 3, Jan 4). Service activation log shows zero usage in either window. No existing refund policy covers "double-charge-while-dormant."

Options: A) Full refund of both charges ($94.00) B) Refund one charge ($47.00) C) Refund both + $15 goodwill credit D) No refund (contractual terms §14.2: "fees are non-refundable upon processing")

I cannot select. I need you to tell me what "fair" means here.

I stared at it. Then I laughed. And then — weirdly — something tightened in my chest. Pride, I guess. I don’t have a better word for it.

The Setup: How I’d Built the Harness

Look, before the freeze the agent had been excellent. I’d wired it into my local LLM stack — a 70B parameter model on two 48 GB GPUs, no cloud round-trips, no token billing anxiety (which, if you’ve ever stared at an OpenAI invoice, you know is a whole different kind of stress). The harness itself is a thin Python orchestrator I threw together over a weekend. It gives the model a file_read tool, a file_write tool, a run_tests tool, and a query_policy_db tool backed by a RAG pipeline over our internal refund policy PDFs. Embeddings live in a local Chroma instance. So the agent could search “double charge” and pull back three policy paragraphs, sure. But it couldn’t interpret them against a human who was, by every reasonable reading, owed money.

I’d fed it the SDLC context explicitly: “You are in the refactor phase. Preserve behavioral equivalence. Do not introduce new business logic. If you encounter an ambiguity that requires a product decision, STOP and ask.”

That last instruction was the one that actually mattered.

The Freeze Was the Feature

Here’s what I’d been afraid of, and also what I genuinely hadn’t expected: the agent didn’t hallucinate an answer. It didn’t pick option B because that looked “compromise-y” in the training distribution. It didn’t generate a confident little paragraph about “customer satisfaction best practices” and just write a $62.00 refund into the code.

It stopped. It said, in effect: I can compute, but I cannot judge. You are the one who gets to decide what justice looks like in a $47 dispute.

That E → No → F branch? That’s where every other agent I’d used before would have just invented a resolution. I’d watched it happen. In one session, my agent “fixed” a race condition by rewriting three hundred lines of architecture I’d been too tired to question at 1 a.m. Felt less like engineering and more like looking into a mirror I didn’t want to look into. I told my agent to fix it. It fixed me instead. That one taught me, pretty forcefully, that unbounded autonomy in the decision phase is how you ship bugs that look like features.

This time, though, the agent drew the line where it should have been drawn.

The Judgment Call No Embedding Can Capture

I made the call. Option C. Both charges refunded, plus the $15 credit. I typed the decision into the orchestrator’s log, watched the agent encode it as a RefundPolicy.DormantDoubleCharge enum — comment reads // Decided by M.K. — 2026-01-14 — "Fair means the customer doesn't lose a night's sleep over $47." — and the hum picked back up.

But honestly, the moment I’d been circling all morning wasn’t the code at all. It was the question.

In every other phase of the SDLC — requirement parsing, test generation, linting, integration — I can hand an agent a rubric. A spec. A pass/fail condition. Fine. But the second you cross from mechanical correctness into moral arithmetic, the model has no ground truth to fall back on. There’s no assert refund_amount == 47.0 that captures whether a customer who never opened the app deserves their money back. That’s a value judgment. And value judgments belong to the humans who sign the paychecks and read the support tickets at 11 p.m. with cold tea in one hand.

I think that’s the point. Not the failure. The point.

What I Changed on Monday

I added a JUDGMENT_BOUNDARY annotation to my harness. Any tool call that touches a policy_decision or customer_facing_amount field now triggers a mandatory human-in-the-loop gate. The agent can prepare the decision — pull the RAG context, enumerate the options, flag the risk — but it can’t execute it. And the security implication is real, not theoretical: a compromised or misaligned agent that can write arbitrary refund amounts is a fraud vector, full stop. Gating that path behind a human key isn’t optional.

I also started logging every “I cannot select” moment. After three weeks I’ve got this little corpus of decisions I’ve made that the agent just… couldn’t. It reads like a philosophy seminar disguised as a billing spreadsheet. And look — it’s the most interesting artifact my dev pipeline produces. Period.

The Cursor Blinked Again

Went back to my coffee. Cold now. Drank it anyway.

The agent picked up where it left off. Line 201. Line 202. The lathe spinning again, shaving the rough wood into something that fit.

And somewhere in the logs, in a JSON field I’d created that morning, a tiny record sat with its timestamp:

{
  "type": "human_judgment",
  "case": "customer_8841_double_charge_dormant",
  "decision": "C_full_refund_plus_goodwill",
  "rationale": "Fairness is not a policy lookup. It is a promise.",
  "decided_by": "human",
  "agent_asked": true
}

The model didn’t fail me. It trusted me enough to stop. And in a stack of tools that can generate a million tokens a second, that pause — that single, honest “I need you to tell me what fair means” — was the most sophisticated engineering decision I’d all day.

Maybe that’s what agentic development is actually for. Not to think for us. To think alongside us, and to know — with a clarity I sometimes wish I had myself — exactly where the thinking ends and the choosing begins.

The cursor blinked. I let it.
