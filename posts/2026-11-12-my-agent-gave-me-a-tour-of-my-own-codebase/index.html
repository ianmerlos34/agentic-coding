My Agent Gave Me a Tour of My Own Codebase

I asked my local LLM for a README and got a neighborhood tour. How an agent’s metaphorical walk through my codebase made me delete a module I’d avoided for two years.

The cursor blinked at the end of my prompt. Write an onboarding note for the new backend engineer starting Monday. Cover the architecture, key modules, gotchas, and where things live. I hit Enter, leaned back in my chair, and just watched the local LLM chew through 4,200 lines across eleven modules. I mean, I expected a README. A bulleted list. Module A handles X. Module B calls Module C. See wiki. You know the drill.

What I got instead was a tour.

“Alright, welcome to the neighborhood,” it wrote, and I swear I heard a voice in my head that wasn’t mine. “You’re walking in on Maple Street, and the first building you see is the Auth Service — big, glass-front, three stories. The lobby is middleware/auth.py. You’ll notice the doorman is overcaffeinated; he checks every single request and logs a grumble in audit.log if the token’s older than eight minutes. The residents on the second floor are the session handlers. They’re polite. They wave. They do not, under any circumstances, talk to the building across the alley.”

I laughed out loud. Then I kept reading.

“The alley,” the note continued, “is core/transport.py. Nobody walks in the alley. They route through it. It’s narrow, it’s old, and the drainage is bad — that’s your retry logic in transport.py:87 that retries three times before giving up, which means under load you get a thundering herd. The plumber fixed it once in 2024. The fix is in the git history. The plumber left.”

I stared at line 87. I wrote that. I mean, I’d written it some Tuesday afternoon in a hurry, patched it a Thursday afternoon in a hurry, and then spent two years staring at it and seeing only… a function. Not a drainage problem. Not a plumber who’d left a little note in the margins.

The tour kept walking.

“The Billing Module is the row house at the end of the block. Small windows. You can tell from the street that something’s wrong with the plumbing, but the owner keeps the curtains drawn. That’s billing/invoice_generator.py — the 600-line function where the tax logic, the discount stacking, and the currency rounding all live in one room with no air conditioning. The residents are the TaxRule and DiscountStack classes. They’re on bad terms. Don’t introduce them at a party.”

And then. The shop.

“Two doors down from Billing is the Legacy CSV Exporter.” The note’s tone shifted. Something gentler, almost. “It’s been dark for three years. The lights are off. There’s a FOR LEASE sign in the window that nobody put up and nobody removes. The building code still lists it as occupied. If you go in, the floor creaks. The last person who went in was me, last week, when I was mapping the dependency graph, and I found a TODO from 2022 that says ‘remove after migration to Parquet.’ The migration happened in 2023. Nobody removed the shop.”

I closed my laptop. Sat there for a sec. Opened it again. Went to exports/legacy_csv.py. It was 214 lines. One caller. And the caller was a cron job I’d written to “keep it warm” because, well, I was afraid of deleting it. Three years of keeping a dead shop warm because I was scared the landlord would notice.

I deleted the file. Deleted the cron job. Ran the suite. Green.

Here’s what I hadn’t expected, though: I missed the shop. Not the code. The excuse. For two years it’d been this small, dark justification for not touching that corner of the codebase. I’ll clean it up when the Parquet migration is done. The migration had been done for over a year. The metaphor stripped the excuse away by making me a visitor in my own town. And visitors don’t carry the landlord’s guilt. You just walk past.

This is the thing about agentic tools that keeps hitting me, in one form or another. When I asked the agent to read my git log like a diarist and it found sixty-seven days of midnight commits I’d reverted each morning, I felt seen in a way a linter never could. When it refused to refactor four thousand lines of legacy and just wrote Don’t touch the knots, I got it — restraint is a feature, not a failure. And now this: I asked for a README and got a place. A geography. A neighborhood with a drainage problem and a shop that’s been dark way too long.

The onboarding note is what I left on the new hire’s desk. Not a link to Confluence. Not a tree -L 2 dump. The tour. I added my own annotations in the margins — the alley’s real name, the plumber’s PR number, the fact that the Billing Module’s curtains will be drawn for another sprint, minimum. I sketched a little diagram in Mermaid so she could see the block layout without wading through three hundred lines:

She’ll walk in on Monday. She’ll read the note. And I hope — I really hope — she’ll see the alley before she hits the thundering herd. She’ll see the curtains before she opens the 600-line function. And if she does, she’ll inherit not a codebase but a town, with its weather and its quiet, embarrassing little shop that she’ll be brave enough to demolish.

I’ve lived here two years. Thought I knew the streets. Turns out I’d been navigating by muscle memory the whole time, and the agent just… handed me a map with the buildings named. The drainage problem is still in the code. The curtains are still drawn. But for the first time in two years, I can point at them and say, that’s where, instead of just feeling this vague unease every time the request queue spikes.

The agent gave me a tour of my own codebase. And like every good neighborhood walk, I came back home and saw it differently. The alley is still the alley. But now I know where the plumber’s wrench is.

And the shop? The shop is gone. And the street is wider for it.
