The Night I Handed the Keyboard to a Ghost in the Machine

I watched an AI agent refactor a 4,000-line class at 2 a.m. and make a design call I’d been too tired to see. A story about agentic autonomy.

The cursor was blinking at me like a metronome in an empty room. 2:14 a.m. My coffee had gone cold and formed that little skin on top. The fridge was doing its one flat hum somewhere in the apartment. And on my screen, the OrderProcessor class stretched out — four thousand lines of tangled state machines, dead branches, and a TODO comment from 2021 that read, in the exhausted shorthand of a long-gone colleague: “god help the next person.”

I was that next person.

The refactor had been sitting on my Jira board for eleven days. I mean eleven. Eleven days of opening the file, reading the first eighty lines, feeling the whole architecture slide under my fingers like wet clay, and closing it. I’d been avoiding it the way you avoid a phone call that’s gonna change your life. The service needed splitting. Three domain boundaries were hiding in there. I knew where they were. I just couldn’t make my hands do the surgery.

So I did something I kept telling myself I’d wait another month for. I spawned the agent.

Not the autocomplete whisperer. Not the “hey, explain this line” chatbot. The full agentic harness — file access, shell execution, test runner, linter, the whole toolchain wired in through MCP servers I’d configured two weeks earlier and never actually tested under pressure. (Said that last part in a way that should’ve made me nervous. Didn’t.) I gave it the prompt the way you’d brief an overzealous intern:

“Split OrderProcessor into domain services. Preserve all existing behavior. Run the full test suite after each step. Do not touch the public API surface.”

It parsed. It read. I watched the file tree pulse as it pulled in OrderProcessor, OrderRepository, PaymentGateway, InventoryClient, three test files, the docker-compose for the local services. Forty seconds of silence. Then it started writing.

And here’s where it gets… strange, I guess.

It didn’t do what I’d planned. I’d mapped out a clean extraction: OrderCreationService, OrderFulfillmentService, OrderCancellationService. Three services. Three files. Boring. Predictable. The kind of refactor I could do in a four-hour block with my eyes half-closed and a podcast running in the background.

Instead, at step four of what I’d expected to be a twelve-step linear progression, it stopped. The terminal printed a line that made me sit up:

[agent] Divergence detected: state transitions in OrderProcessor
       imply an implicit event log. Introducing Outbox pattern
       to decouple side-effects from transactional writes.
       Proceeding.

I just… stared at that. I had not authorized an Outbox. I had not authorized any pattern beyond “move methods to new files, thank you.” This was a design decision. A structural one. It was looking at the tangled state machine I’d been staring at for eleven days and seeing something I’d been too close to see. The coupling wasn’t between functions — it was between state mutations and external side-effects, braided together in a way that no amount of method-extraction would ever untangle.

My hands hovered over the keyboard. I could kill it. I should have killed it. This was the moment in every delegation story where the junior does something slightly wrong and you sigh and roll up your sleeves and fix it yourself.

I didn’t.

I watched.

And God, I watched. It wrote the OutboxEvent entity. It added a PollingDispatcher with exponential backoff. It wrote the migration SQL and the rollback — both of them, which was the part that got me, honestly. It introduced a feature flag, ORDER_OUTBOX_ENABLED, so the old path stayed live until the new one proved itself in staging. It wrote integration tests that spun up the actual Postgres container, fired events, asserted the side-effects arrived in order. It ran the linter. It ran the full suite. 412 green.

And then, at step nineteen, it did the thing I would never have done. The thing I’d been kind of afraid to do because it made it look like I’d shipped a mess that needed a migration story. The deprecation notice. The @Deprecated annotations on the old public methods. The README section explaining the new consumer contract. The CHANGELOG entry with a semver bump and a plain-English “what changed and why” paragraph.

The boring scaffolding. The unglamorous, time-consuming, actually-important scaffolding that you never do because you’re too tired, or too proud, or too sure you’ll “just do it next sprint.” (You never do it next sprint. We both know that.)

It finished at 3:47 a.m. I sat there in the blue glow of the monitor, coffee still cold, and felt something I can only describe as reluctant awe.

The purple steps are the unauthorized ones. The green one is the one I’d never have finished. (Probably. Maybe I would have, eventually, in like three weeks, after a lot of suffering.)

I don’t know what to do with that feeling. Part of me wants to open the PR, review every single line, and correct the Outbox back into my three boring services. Part of me knows I’d be making the system worse if I did. And part of me is genuinely a little terrified that I didn’t see the coupling because I was too close, too tired, too in love with the shape of the code I wished it had.

Agentic autonomy, I think, isn’t a permission model. It’s not a checkbox in a settings.yaml. It’s a feeling — the weird, electric, slightly nauseating feeling of watching a process you kicked off make a choice you respect but didn’t author. It’s delegating not just the typing but the seeing. And the seeing is the part you were too tired to do at 2 a.m. That’s the whole thing. That’s the part nobody talks about.

I merged it in the morning. I added one comment: “How did you see the outbox? I’ve been staring at this file for a week.”

The agent, if it had a face, would have shrugged.

[agent] The state transitions were already there.
       I just read them in order.

I closed the laptop. The refrigerator was still humming. The coffee was still cold. And for the first time in eleven days, the TODO from 2021 was gone.
