The Day My Agent Stopped Saying ‘Done’ and Said ’Why?

Four questions from my local LLM agent caught a silent architecture bug my spec would have shipped. Why curiosity beats autonomy in AI-assisted development.

The cursor blinked. My coffee had gone cold. And the agent—my little, tireless, local-LLM-powered collaborator sitting in the terminal like a junior dev who’d memorized every RFC in the building—had just stared back at me with four questions.

Four. Questions.

I’d typed the spec in. Clean. Unambiguous. “Refactor the payment retry handler to use exponential backoff with jitter, cap at five attempts, emit a structured log on each retry, and return a 429 with a Retry-After header on exhaustion.” Seventy-two words. A compiler’s dream. The kind of ticket you’d paste into Jira and never think about again.

I expected the familiar cascade: file opens, diff streams, tests run, green. Then that quiet, smug little Done.

Instead, the terminal printed:

  1. The spec says “cap at five attempts,” but the existing CircuitBreaker in middleware/ already caps at three. Do you want the retry handler to override the breaker, or should the breaker be the single source of truth?
  2. “Structured log on each retry”—the current logger emits to stdout in a flat format. The observability stack (Grafana → Loki) expects JSON with a trace_id field. Should I add the trace context, or is that out of scope?
  3. The 429 path: your auth/middleware sets its own Retry-After for rate-limiting. If both fire in the same request cycle, the second header silently overwrites the first. Which one wins, or do they need to compose?
  4. You said “exponential backoff with jitter.” Full jitter, equal jitter, or decorrelated jitter? The three produce very different load curves under burst traffic, and the existing timeout config in config.yaml assumes a base of 200 ms. Do you want me to recompute the ceiling, or leave it?

I stared at the screen. Afternoon light was going amber through the blinds, catching the dust motes above my monitor. Somewhere in the next room, my partner was humming a song that didn’t exist. (Or maybe it did. I’ve never asked.)

My first instinct—my instinct, the one drilled into me by a decade of “write the ticket, get the PR, ship it”—was to type: “Just pick reasonable defaults and go.”

I didn’t.

Because question three had a hairline crack in my architecture. A silent one. The kind that would ship, pass CI, and then bite us at 3 a.m. on a Black Friday spike when the auth middleware’s rate limiter and the payment retry handler both tried to set Retry-After and the client got a 0-second delay. The kind of bug I’d have caused if I’d just said “done” to my own spec six months ago.

I sat back. Poured the cold coffee down the sink. Made a new one.

“What’s the load curve difference between full and decorrelated?” I asked.

The agent pulled the relevant section from its RAG index—three paragraphs from a 2019 AWS blog post I’d ingested into the local vector store last month, plus the actual timeout values from config.yaml—and walked me through the math. Not a wall of text. A short paragraph, a little table, and then: “Under your current 200 ms base, decorrelated jitter means the fifth attempt lands somewhere between 3.2 s and 6.4 s. Full jitter means 1.6 s to 6.4 s. If your downstream service tolerates 5 s but not 6.5 s, you’ll start seeing 429s cascade into 502s.”

I felt the floor tilt slightly. Not dramatically. Just enough.

Here’s the thing. All those months of “harness this, prompt that, pipe the context through the MCP server and get a diff”—I’d been treating the agent like a compiler. I hand it source code. It hands me an executable. If the executable crashes, that’s the bug. Compiler’s blameless. It did what I told it, right?

But the agent wasn’t a compiler. It was the colleague two desks over. The one who reads your spec, raises an eyebrow, and goes, “Wait—did you mean this or that? Because if it’s this, the auth middleware is going to eat your header.”

The difference isn’t intelligence. It’s posture. A compiler assumes the spec is complete. A collaborator assumes the spec is a starting point, and that the gaps between what I wrote and what the system actually does are where the real bugs live.

The next three hours were the most productive debugging I’ve done all year. Not because the agent wrote brilliant code—though the backoff implementation was clean and the structured logging was exactly what Loki needed. But because we argued. Small, sharp arguments. “What if the breaker and the retry handler disagree on the cap?” “Then one of them is lying. Which one do you trust?” I chose the breaker. The agent updated the spec in the prompt history, so future runs wouldn’t re-litigate it. We iterated. Caught the Retry-After composition issue. Picked decorrelated jitter because my partner, passing the door with her imaginary song, said “five seconds feels too long for a checkout” and I agreed.

In every way that mattered, it was a senior-engineer pairing session. Except the senior engineer ran on a 13B parameter model I’d quantized to 4-bit and loaded onto my M2 Max, and it never needed a coffee break.

I’ve thought a lot about that afternoon since. About how I’d spent months building the plumbing—the harness, the tool definitions, the MCP endpoints, the RAG pipeline—while treating the agent at the other end like a tsc command. In goes code. Out goes behavior. No questions. No opinions. No “hey, I think your circuit breaker is lying.”

The plumbing was necessary. But it was never the point.

The point was the conversation. The point was those four questions that almost made me type “Just pick reasonable defaults” and walk away with a subtly broken payment path and a very confident Done.

I still hand specs to agents. I still use the harness, the local LLM, the vector store. But now, when the terminal prints a question instead of a diff, I don’t feel interrupted.

I feel addressed.

And the day I handed the keyboard to a ghost in the machine, watching it make a design call I’d been too tired to see, I thought autonomy was the goal. I was wrong. Autonomy without curiosity is just a very fast compiler. The real magic—the thing that turned a 4,000-line refactor into a 4,000-line improvement—wasn’t the speed. It was the questions.

The day my agent stopped saying “Done” and started saying “Why?” was the day it stopped being a tool and became a teammate.

And I, for the first time in a long while, stopped being the only one in the room.
