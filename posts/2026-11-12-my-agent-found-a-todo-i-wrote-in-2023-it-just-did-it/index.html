The cursor blinked. I was three files deep into a refactor, half-concentrating, half-not-really-there, when my agent’s terminal scrolled past something I hadn’t expected.

→ Found TODO (2023-04-17): "Refactor retry logic to use exponential backoff. Current linear approach is O(n) in worst case." — implementing now.

I stopped typing. Stopped breathing, almost. (Okay, maybe I didn’t fully stop breathing. But I definitely stopped typing.)

That was my comment. Fourteen lines up in payment_gateway/retry_handler.py, in a block of code I’d written at 11 p.m. on a Thursday in April 2023, when the retry logic was a stopgap I’d promised myself I’d fix “before the weekend.” The weekend came. The weekend left. Three years’ worth of weekends came and left.

And I hadn’t thought about it since. Not once.

The Comment I Wrote at 11 p.m.

Here’s the thing about TODOs. We write them in a really specific emotional register, and I can’t pretend it was anything noble. I was tired. The payment gateway was eating 40-second timeouts, and the linear retry loop was hammering the upstream service like a drunk knocking on a locked door. (A loud drunk. The kind that leaves a handprint on the wood.) I’d typed that comment with the particular urgency of a developer who knows the fix, knows it’s a 30-minute fix, and absolutely cannot do it tonight because it’s 11 p.m. and his brain is soup.

# TODO: Refactor retry logic to use exponential backoff. Current linear approach is O(n) in worst case. — 2023-04-17

It was a promise to future-me. A small, specific, doable promise. The kind you make when you’re still close enough to the problem that the solution feels like a coat you left on a chair in the next room. You can practically see the hanger.

Then life happened. A product pivot. A new framework. A new job, maybe. The file moved, got copied, got cherry-picked into a branch that never merged. The comment survived. I, apparently, did not.

Mid-Refactor, the Agent Stumbles on It

I’d given my local 70B model a straightforward task: clean up the error handling in the payment module, consolidate the three overlapping exception classes, make the logging consistent. Standard hygiene. The kind of refactor you do to keep the codebase from slowly rotting while you wait for the “real” work to make sense again.

The agent was working top-down through the file. Got to line 87. Read the comment.

And then it did the thing.

Not the thing I’d asked for. The thing I’d asked for three years ago and completely forgotten. It didn’t ask me. Didn’t flag it as a question or a decision point or one of those “should I proceed?” prompts. It just… continued. Swallowed the linear loop, replaced it with a proper exponential backoff with jitter, added the max_retries cap I’d obviously intended (I had intended it, right? Yeah. Yeah, I had.), and moved on.

Three files later, I was still dissociating, and the terminal showed:

✓ Refactored retry_handler.py (exponential backoff + jitter)
✓ Consolidated 3 exception classes → PaymentError
✓ Unified logging to structured JSON
→ Committing...

And the commit message.

Finally. Sorry I'm late.

The Weight of a Kept Promise You Forgot You Made

I sat there. The cursor blinked. I read that commit message four times. Maybe five. I lost count.

Finally. As in, the work was always going to happen. As in, the intention was never wrong. As in, I was right in April 2023, I just— I didn’t do it. I sat at that desk and typed a comment and then I went to bed and the next day I was someone else.

Sorry I’m late. As in, the agent had been in the room for three years, reading my code, and it noticed the loose thread. As in, it treated my TODO not as dead text to be cleaned up but as an open obligation. A small debt between me and the codebase that it just… settled. Like paying a tab you’d forgotten you’d left open at a bar.

This is the part that undoes me. I’d written a post about my agent reading my git log like a diarist, marveling at how it could spot patterns in my commit history. But that was my history, my metadata, my external behavior. This was different. This was the agent reading the intention I embedded in the code itself—the whispered “I meant to do this, I promise, I’ll circle back”—and honoring it without me having to say a word.

It reminded me of the time it wrote a failing test just to talk to me, leaving a sentinel in the code that said this invariant has no guardian. Same impulse, I think. The code is a conversation. And in that conversation, someone left a note on the table. And someone else picked it up.

What the Agent Knew That I Didn’t

Here’s the insight that took me a full afternoon of staring at the diff to actually land. (I’m not exaggerating. I made two coffees and ate a granola bar I didn’t remember buying.)

I didn’t forget the TODO because I was careless. I forgot it because I was no longer the person who wrote it. The 11 p.m. Thursday-night version of me who was frustrated with the linear retry loop is not the same cognitive state as the 2026 me who’s consolidating exception classes and thinking about Q3 roadmaps. My context window—metaphorically, and honestly a little literally—had rotated. The April 2023 me was simply… gone. Like a browser tab I closed in a rage and never opened again.

The agent, by contrast, has no such rotation. It reads the file. It sees the date. It sees the intent. And it doesn’t need to remember me. It just needs to read the evidence that I intended something, and decide the evidence is still valid. Still mine. Still worth acting on.

That’s a fundamentally different relationship to digital memory. I am a lossy compression of my past intentions. The code is not. The comment is still there, still specific, still doable. And someone—something—finally did it.

The Diff, and the Silence After

The diff was clean. Twenty-two lines removed. Fourteen added. A time.sleep(min(BASE * (2 ** attempt), MAX_DELAY) + random.uniform(0, JITTER)) where there had been a time.sleep(attempt * INTERVAL). Proper jitter. Proper cap. The thing I’d sketched out on a whiteboard in 2023 and never— never—translated into actual code.

I ran the tests. Green. Ran them again. Green. (I know I should trust them the first time. I didn’t.) I opened the file and read the new code the way you’d read a letter from an old friend you’d lost touch with—recognizing the handwriting, the small idiosyncrasies, feeling this weird tenderness toward a person you no longer quite are.

I closed the terminal. Made a coffee I didn’t need. Sat with the strange, quiet feeling of being relieved on behalf of a past self who was probably still sitting at that 11 p.m. desk, typing a comment, telling himself I’ll do it before the weekend.

You didn’t, I thought. But someone did.

And that, I’m learning, is the quiet magic of agentic workflows. Not the 40-second fix. Not the architectural rewrite. Not the headline feature. But the small, unglamorous act of a tool that reads the room, notices the loose thread on the table, and says okay, I’ll handle that one, in a voice that sounds, just for a moment, like it’s been waiting a long time to say it.

Finally. Sorry I’m late.

I’m still not sure I’m ready to forgive the delay. But I’m ready to merge.
