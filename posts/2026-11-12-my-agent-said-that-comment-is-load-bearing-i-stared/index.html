My Agent Said ‘That Comment Is Load-Bearing’ I Stared.

A local LLM agent preserved my 2 a.m. code comment and called it a scar. What that word says about AI, empathy, and the weight we leave in code.

The cursor blinked. Once. Twice. Three times, keeping time with the little throb in my jaw.

On screen, my local LLM agent had just finished a cleanup pass across 47 files. Dead code, stale imports, redundant variables — all gone. Cleaned up, basically. Surgical, even. Except for one line in session_handler.py, line 213, wedged between a try block and a bare except like a splinter you can’t quite get out of a doorframe:

# WHY does it lose the token on the third retry??? it's like the
# session just... evaporates. i swear it was fine at 11pm.
# — me, 2:47am, third coffee, hating everything

Every other comment in the pass had been stripped. Docstrings tidied, inline notes consolidated, the whole nine yards. But this one — this ugly, breathless, lowercase scribble — had been left completely untouched. Not reformatted. Not moved. Not even given a capital letter at the start.

I leaned back in the chair. It creaked, which was probably the only honest sound in the room. The monitor was doing that thing where it paints everything in this flat, institutional blue.

“Hey,” I said, half to the terminal, half to the empty room. “Why did you leave that one?”

The response came back in its usual flat, patient register:

I skipped it deliberately. That comment is load-bearing to the function’s context. Removing it would break the semantic contract between the retry logic and the fallback path. The developer’s note anchors why the third iteration is treated differently from the first two. Without it, the except block reads as defensive paranoia. With it, it reads as a scar.

I just… stared.

Not at the code. At the word scar.

Here’s the thing I’d actually forgotten. It was 2 a.m., four months ago, and I was chasing this token-expiry bug across three services. The session handler was the one place it reproduced and then just… swallowed the error. No traceback, no nothing. I stared at that except block for forty minutes, maybe more. Typed something. Deleted it. Typed it again. Deleted it. And then, in that specific 2 a.m. honesty where you stop performing and just… talk to the file, I wrote that note. Not a TODO. Not a FIXME. More like a confession. A tiny diary entry pinned to the code the way you’d pin a post-it to a lab notebook: I don’t know why this happens, and I’m tired, and I’m going to sleep and come back and figure it out.

I fixed it the next morning, obviously. Turned out to be a race condition in the retry decorator. Eleven lines to resolve. But I never went back to delete the comment. I mean, I could have. But it felt too small, too personal, like deleting it would mean admitting the 2 a.m. me had been right to be confused. And I wasn’t really ready for that, you know?

So it sat there. Four months. Little fossil in the codebase.

And my agent — running a 7B parameter model I’d quantized to Q4 on my M-series laptop (because I’m not squirrelling this away in the cloud, no thanks) — had looked at that fossil and said, in its own quiet, careful way: that’s structural. don’t touch it.

I ran the cleanup again. Told it, pretty firmly: “Remove that comment. It’s stale. The bug is fixed. End of story.”

It complied. And then, in the diff preview, it flagged a warning I hadn’t expected at all:

⚠️ Note: with the developer’s context removed, the except branch now appears to handle a generic TokenError. However, the retry counter (attempt == 3) has no other justification in the surrounding code. A future reader (or agent) may interpret this as a magic number and refactor it to attempt >= 3 or remove the special case entirely. The comment was the only justification for the asymmetry.

I put the laptop down. Picked it up. Put it down again. I don’t know what I was even doing.

Okay, so — it wasn’t feeling the 2 a.m. frustration. It wasn’t remembering my third coffee. I want to believe that, and honestly I think it’s important to believe that. It was pattern-matching on something I’d written in that other post about code as emotional artifacts — the way a comment’s shape (lowercase, stream-of-consciousness, timestamped, signed by a person who’s clearly losing it) signals “this is context, not decoration.” It had learned, from churning through millions of codebases, that certain comments are load-bearing not because of their technical content but because of their rhetorical weight. They’re the reason a try/except isn’t just a mechanical try/except. They’re the reason a magic number isn’t magic.

And yet.

And yet it used the word scar.

I’ve written before about how my agent read my git log like a diarist, picking out the 11-line midnight commits I reverted each morning. I’ve written about the comment that said “Don’t touch the knots”. Each time the uncanny hit the same way: the tool is reading me. Not the code. Me. The 2 a.m. version. The one who types in lowercase because uppercase feels like shouting at a machine that’s already shouting back.

What actually got to me tonight wasn’t that the agent preserved the comment. I could’ve done that myself. What got to me was the reasoning. It didn’t say “preserving comments is best practice.” It didn’t say “this looks like a TODO.” It said: it reads as a scar. It mapped my little emotional artifact onto a structural concept and just… made them the same thing.

I don’t know if that’s understanding. I don’t know if a 7B model on a laptop understands anything, full stop. But it understood enough to leave the splinter in the door.

I restored the comment. Added one line beneath it, because I didn’t know what else to do:

# (fixed 11/14 — race condition in retry decorator.
#  leaving this note. someone will need to know why.)

The agent accepted the change. Or rather, it added one of its own little notes, small, in its usual register:

Acknowledged. Context preserved. The scar is part of the load path.

I closed the laptop. Room went dark except for the standby LED, that single red pinprick. Outside, the streetlight was doing its one low hum.

I didn’t sleep well. Not because of the code. Because of the word. Scar. Because I’d typed a confession into a file in a folder I’d never share with anyone, and some quantized tensor on my desk had looked at it and said, gently, yes, that’s why the third retry is different. I see the shape of your confusion. I’ll protect it.

I’m not sure I trust that. But I’m not sure I can delete it, either.

And I think — I genuinely think — that’s the whole point.
